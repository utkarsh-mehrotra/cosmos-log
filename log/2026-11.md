## 2026-11-05 — NASA Science
**Copyright:** Public Domain

![NASA Science](../assets/2026-11-05.png)

> It is time to say goodbye to NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory. After nearly 22 years of
> service, the satellite re-entered Earth's atmosphere on November 4 at 7:39 EST over the North
> Sumatra region of Indonesia. The featured image is a mosaic created in 2018 using the first one
> million images captured by the satellite's Ultraviolet/Optical Telescope. Swift had a combination of
> instruments, including an X-ray telescope and a gamma-ray telescope, that worked together to enable
> humanity to study, in unprecedented detail, the fastest and most energetic explosions in the
> universe. The most recent solar maximum accelerated the decay of the satellite's orbit. An
> innovative mission to rescue Swift by boosting its orbit was not successful. Swift burned on reentry
> like a shooting star, but the impressive data acquired by the telescope over its mission will
> continue to bring light to the mysteries of the cosmos for years to come. May it keep inspiring new
> missions to boldly go into space, explore new data and seek out new science.

## 2026-11-04 — NASA Science
**Copyright:** Public Domain

![NASA Science](../assets/2026-11-04.png)

> “I see” IC 342, the Hidden Galaxy! Today’s image peers through the plane of the Milky Way, through
> all its gas, dust and stars, to unveil this spiral galaxy. IC 342 (or Caldwell 5) is one of the
> brightest members of the IC 342/Maffei group of galaxies. Located about 10 million light years away,
> this group is quite close to the Local Group that includes our Milky Way. Inaccurate accounting of
> the crowded and dusty Milky Way foreground has led to misunderstandings in IC 342’s history,
> including the theory that the galaxy was ejected from the Local Group. IC 342 has its own dust
> intermittently blocking the glow of hydrogen gas, indicating the presence of bright, young stars.
> Magnetic field maps of IC 342 reveal that magnetic fields may contribute substantially to one of the
> biggest galactic puzzles: how do spiral arms form?Tomorrow's picture: open space

## 2026-11-02 — NASA Science
**Copyright:** Public Domain

![NASA Science](../assets/2026-11-02.png)

>  Did this meteor come from that comet? Probably not. Although many meteors do originate from comets,
> there are thousands of comets that have visited the inner Solar System recently, and matching
> meteors to them can be tricky. And to be clear, the pictured comet is far behind the streaking
> meteor. Comet C/2025 R3 (PanSTARRS) was captured from Hautacam, France in April when the giant space
> iceberg sported impressive ion (blue) and dust tails. Sand-sized rocky debris that enters Earth’s
> atmosphere typically appears as meteors, while the colorful foreground streak here may indicate a
> complicated composition. This month three notable meteor showers will reach their peaks. Over the
> next few days, the Southern Taurids will be reaching their apex, while the Northern Taurids will top
> out about a week later. Both originate from Comet 2P/Encke. November is most famous for the Leonids
> Meteor Shower, though, which originates from Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle and will reach its crescendo in
> about two weeks.Tomorrow's picture: a hidden gem

## 2026-11-01 — NASA Science
**Copyright:** Public Domain

![NASA Science](../assets/2026-11-01.png)

> What’s causing this unusually long cloud on Mars? Known as the Arsia Mons Elongated Cloud (AMEC) and
> first recorded by NASA’s Viking Orbiter 2 in 1977, the strange cloud has been imaged in greater
> detail more recently by ESA’s robotic Mars Express spacecraft. The featured image is from 2018. The
> cloud forms only during spring and summer seasons and then only during morning hours. Made of water-
> ice, the most familiar process that causes cloud crystals to form around bits of dust would fail due
> to the thinness and lack of dust high in the Martian atmosphere. Leading speculation holds that dust
> is not involved. After the cloud forms near the volcano Arsia Mons, wind blows the cloud downstream
> — creating a tail that can extend over 1500 kilometers. Efforts to better understand AMECs will
> continue, an understanding that may bolster a more complete view of cloud formation on
> Earth.Tomorrow's picture: eyeing a cat
